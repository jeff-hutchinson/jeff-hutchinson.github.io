To you, it is just a pristine sheet inside a wooden frame. You spent good money on it, placed it neatly in the hive, and now you are tapping your foot wondering why we are ignoring it.

You see an empty box that needs filling. We see a massive architectural project that requires a strict budget, the perfect weather, and a collective agreement among forty thousand sisters.

If you want to understand why we aren't drawing out that comb, you have to stop looking at the calendar and start thinking like a bee. We don't build on demand; we build on necessity and abundance. Here is what we actually need before we can start sweating wax.

## The Construction Checklist: Our Core Requirements

We don't just decide to build on a whim. Making comb is the most energy-intensive chore in the hive. To pull it off, three strict conditions must be met simultaneously:

*   **A Torrent of Liquid Carbs:** Producing just one pound of wax requires us to consume roughly six to eight pounds of honey or heavy nectar. If there isn't an active nectar flow outside—or a massive supply of heavy syrup coming from above—the factory shuts down. We cannot afford to bankrupt our winter food reserves just to build empty rooms.
*   **A Youth Movement:** Wax production is a young bee’s game. The specialized wax-secreting glands on our abdomens are most active when we are between 12 and 18 days old. If a colony is top-heavy with older foragers and short on nurse bees, we simply don't have the workforce to sweat the wax, no matter how much syrup you give us.
*   **Crowding and Warmth:** To shape wax, we have to festoon—linking our legs together to form living chains, raising our collective body temperature to a sweltering 95°F (35°C). If the hive is weak, or if there is too much empty space, we cannot hold that heat. We will only build if we are crowded enough to need the space and strong enough to keep it warm.

## The Material Matters: Real Wax vs. The Plastic Wall

Beekeepers love to argue about what kind of frames to buy, but they rarely ask us how we feel about it. The material you drop into our hive completely changes how we approach the job.

*   **100% Beeswax Foundation / Starter Strips:** When you give us pure, fragrant beeswax—or even just an empty frame with a real wax starter strip—we recognize it instantly as home. It smells right, it feels right, and it acts as a structural guide. We can instantly anchor our feet to it and start extending the cells. It takes less convincing to get us moving on real wax.
*   **The Plastic Foundation Dilemma:** Then there is plastic. To us, bare plastic is an alien, slippery, offensive material. We cannot grip it easily, and we certainly cannot build on it as-is. The only way we will ever draw comb on a plastic sheet is if it is coated in a heavy, generous layer of real beeswax. If the manufacturer (or you) only gave it a "micro-coat" or a light spray, we will look at it like a sheet of drywall with no studs. We will crawl all over it, ignore it, and wait until we are so utterly desperate for space that we have no choice but to try and scrape together enough wax to make it usable.

If you must use plastic, make sure it is heavily waxed. Otherwise, you are asking us to build a skyscraper on a sheet of ice.

## The Solstice and the Dearth: The Biological Hard Stop

Many of you get frustrated in July and August because we stop drawing comb. You assume because it is hot and sunny, it is prime building weather.

We see it differently.

Once the summer solstice passes, the days begin to shorten. To us, this is the ultimate cosmic alarm clock. It signals that winter is coming. Our entire priority shifts from expansion to consolidation.

When the mid-summer dearth hits and the wildflowers dry up, our incoming resources plummet. If we were to spend our precious summer nectar drawing out fresh comb in late July or August, we would starve by December. We are hardwired to hoard our honey in the existing comb, pack the brood nest with pollen, and raise fat winter bees. Unless there is an extraordinary fall nectar flow, the wax factory is closed for the year by mid-summer.

## The Plastic Ceiling: Why We Hate Queen Excluders

We often see beekeepers drop a brand-new box of plastic foundation directly on top of a queen excluder, only to wonder why we treat it like a brick wall.

Think about it from our perspective: you have placed a physical barrier between us and our mother, forced us to squeeze through a tight mesh, and expected us to climb into a cold, empty, drafty attic to start a massive construction project.

We like to build from the center of our home outward and upward, maintaining a tight, warm cluster. If you isolate that empty box behind an excluder before we have even started building, we will view that space as "outside" the home. We won't risk our energy or heat to cross the barrier just to work on cold plastic.

## Are We Unable, or Unwilling?

The next time you open our hive and see untouched foundation, ask yourself these questions from our point of view:

1.  Is there a heavy flow of nectar or syrup coming in right now?
2.  Are there thousands of young bees hatching to do the heavy lifting?
3.  Is the hive crowded enough that we genuinely need the room?
4.  Did you give us a quality surface that smells and feels like a hive?

If the answer to any of these is no, we aren't being stubborn. We are just being smart.
